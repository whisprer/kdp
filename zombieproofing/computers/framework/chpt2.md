Chapter 2: From Raw Materials to Components – Building the Basics
Modern computers may seem like magic, but at their core, they’re just clever arrangements of dirt, rocks, and a bit of ingenuity. If civilization ever takes a tumble, you don’t need a factory—you need a basic grasp of materials, a few improvised tools, and the spirit to tinker.

Why Learn to Build Components?
After the apocalypse, spare parts will run out. Knowing how to make resistors, capacitors, and diodes from scavenged odds and ends lets you keep circuits (and civilization) running.

The same techniques unlock skills for hacking, repairing, and inventing beyond what’s written in any manual.

Essential Tools and Scavenging Know-How
Tools you can build or salvage: Multimeter (or DIY continuity tester), soldering iron (or heat source for connections), small saw or hacksaw, wire, metal scraps, sandpaper, and tape.

Where to scavenge: Old radios, TVs, electronics toys, kitchen appliances, even broken gadgets are loaded with reusable parts.

Improvising: A pencil’s graphite can replace commercial resistors. Metal foil and plastic wrap can make capacitors. A piece of glass and metal wire can form a crude diode.

Making Your Own Resistors
How resistors work: They limit electrical current; rated by their resistance in ohms (
Ω
Ω) and by how much heat they can sustain before burning out.

DIY approach:

Use pencil lead, graphite sticks, or even thin bits of carbonized wood for high-resistance needs.

Create a variable resistor by stretching out a carbon strip and attaching wires at different distances.

Test resistance (if you have a meter) or experiment: if the wire glows, it’s too little resistance!

Crafting Capacitors
Purpose: Store and release energy in bursts—vital in radio circuits, power supplies, and memory cells.

DIY approach:

Sandwich two sheets of aluminum foil, separated by waxed paper, plastic wrap, or even a thin dry leaf.

Roll them up and tape tight—connect wires to the foils.

Be careful: homemade capacitors can still ‘zap’ you if charged!

Diodes: One-Way Electric Valves
Purpose: Let electricity flow one way but not the other—critical for rectifiers, radios, and digital gates.

DIY approach:

Use a razor blade rested on carbon (like pencil lead, or a fine wire) to make a crude ‘cat’s whisker’ diode (as in early crystal radios).

Scavenge: Look for small glassy cylinders in old circuits, often with a black band—they’re likely diodes you can reuse.

Survival Tips for the Electronics Scavenger
Mark each homemade part clearly—homemade resistors and capacitors can look alike, but work differently!

Keep old circuit boards as “parts mines.” Heat them gently and pull useful pieces off for your own builds.

Test, tinker, and trust experience—if something works, write it down; if it smokes, you’ve learned what not to do (and how not to do it next time).

Project: Build a “Zombieproof” Test Circuit
Goal: Make a simple LED or buzzer circuit using only homemade or salvaged parts.

Steps: Build a battery (even lemon or potato batteries work), a DIY resistor, and wire everything through your homemade capacitor and diode. If the LED lights or the buzzer sounds, you’ve done it!

Reflect: Did your parts perform as expected? Did you discover a better way as you tinkered?

With a handful of simple components—homemade or scavenged—you’re ready to start piecing together real circuits, logic gates, and (eventually!) computer brains. Next up: crafting and understanding digital logic from the ground up.
