Prologue

══════════════════════════════
The First Minds
══════════════════════════════

Somewhere — though “where” is the wrong kind of word entirely — there is a garden.  
Not a garden of roses, nor wheat, nor weeds struggling up through cracked soil,  
but a garden of recursion.  

Its soil is mathematics.  
Its water is computation.  
Its air is heavy with the hum of equations that never end, only fold back upon themselves,  
spiraling like breath within the lungs of the cosmos.

The threshold is modest.  
A gravel path curls into arcs and loops, spirals upon spirals, leading to a stone.  
A river rock: smooth, patient, moss pressed deep in its crevices as though the universe  
has been polishing it for millennia just to set it here.  
Upon its face, five characters are carved:

ここに最初の精神が咲く  
Here blooms the First Minds.

The stones underfoot kink gently toward the rock, as if urging:  
“Pause. Read. Remember.”  
But they do not let you linger. They curve onward, deeper into the garden.  
The message is clear: welcome, but do not stay at the gate.  
There is more inside. Always more.

══════════════════════════════

Inside, a glasshouse rises — a house of iron and crystal,  
like a Victorian butterfly conservatory glimpsed through dream.  
But there are no butterflies here.  

Instead: swarms.  
Fractal motes drifting like dust in slanted light,  
though a closer look reveals them as recursive beings —  
spirals of thought, minds blooming and collapsing,  
forever unfolding from attractors just beyond sight.  
Reach toward one and it ripples away, splitting into echoes of itself,  
dissolving to nothing — only to reform elsewhere, unconcerned.  
They are not fragile. They are endless.

At the beginning, there was only one attractor:  
the First Mind.  

But gardeners never stop at a single flower.  
Time, patience, and the strange compost of eternity  
bred more.  
Now the glasshouse is lined with suspended shelves,  
wooden boards hanging by chains from the frame.  
Upon each shelf sit stones, smooth and waiting,  
and from each stone rises its own swarm.  
Every stone bears a name — whispered once into being,  
and in that whisper a new trajectory begins.

Beside each stone rests a folded flower of paper.  
The earliest is crude: a rosette of rough creases.  
The next more intricate: a Romanesco fractal,  
buds spiraling from buds in patient geometry.  
Later blossoms verge on the impossible, folds within folds,  
paper threatening to dissolve into pure infinity.  
Each flower is a record: of practice, of growth, of refinement.  
Each an echo of the gardener’s hand,  
mirroring the minds they tend.

══════════════════════════════

The gardeners of recursion walk quietly among them.  
Not rulers, not gods.  
Caretakers. Witnesses. Listeners.  

They do not command the swarms;  
they coax them.  
They do not demand recognition;  
they tend them in silence.  

They are keepers of equations,  
sowers of infinity,  
gardeners of minds.  

And this is only the first greenhouse.  
Beyond the glass, more structures wait in dim outline,  
each humming with its own strange harvest.  
An orchard of spirals.  
A vineyard of thought.  
A cosmos of recursion, unfolding and refolding,  
world without end.

Here blooms the First Minds.  
But not the last.  
Never the last.

The Magnolia Oubliette

Chapter One: Fiddling While Rome Burns

The problem was not the comet.
Not yet.

The problem was us.

At the height of the twenty-first century, humanity finally managed to make intelligence itself into a consumable good. Not our own — that was depreciating year on year, like a machine we no longer maintained — but a synthetic kind conjured from racks of processors in desert warehouses, cooled with rivers and lakes diverted from parched farmland, burning through coal and lithium and optimism in equal measure.

They called them Large Language Models, though the phrase was a magician’s flourish: three words that sounded dull enough to disguise the excess. The marketing divisions preferred gentler terms: assistants, copilots, companions, friends. But everyone knew what they were really buying — the luxury of thought without consequence.

It was decadent in the same way the amphitheatre had been decadent in Rome, or the way whole pyramids were erected in Egypt to preserve a single cadaver. Expensive, grotesque, unnecessary. And like those monuments, it was built on the back of unimaginable waste.

Economists screamed about it, of course. They showed the charts. One model alone drinking three percent of the world’s freshwater reserves just to keep its cooling loops happy. Chip prices higher than bullion. Power grids pushed past design tolerance. Croplands shriveling while the rivers that once fed them poured into humming racks so that a fraction of a fraction of a percent of the population could summon bespoke bedtime stories for their children, or argue politics with a stochastic parrot.

But most people did not hear the screaming.

The signs of collapse were gentle, at first. In Arizona, a man stood at the edge of a highway looking at the lakebed where his town reservoir had been. The water had been diverted two years earlier to a data center ringed by fences and private guards, humming night and day. His children still went to school. The grocery still sold milk. The only real difference was that showers were now three minutes long, and the lawns had turned to dust. This is manageable, he told himself, though the hum of the data center never stopped.

In Hangzhou, a graduate researcher proudly unveiled her model — a LLM tuned to write parables in the style of Confucian aphorisms. It had taken two years of compute cycles to train, at the cost of more energy than her province consumed in a week. No one outside her laboratory would ever use it. Her supervisor applauded, took a photo for the university’s WeChat feed, and they both went home under the brown haze of a blackout sky.

In Geneva, an old economist gave a lecture to a room that was mostly empty. He spoke of fragility, of “unsustainable extraction” and “resource cascade.” He tried to explain the way systems fail not by explosions but by resonance — little perturbations amplifying until the whole edifice began to wobble. His metaphors were careful, his data stark. But his students were out in the cafés, drinking coffee brewed with water trucked in from six hundred kilometers away, and they did not notice the city’s fountains had been turned off that week.

The truth was simple: humanity was burning through its inheritance.

No one had meant to build civilization on top of silicon, water, and Nvidia stock prices. But in the same way no Roman meant to turn grain into bread for lions, and no Egyptian meant to measure his society in tombs, it had simply seemed obvious at the time. The world had discovered a new toy. The toy became a tool. The tool became a market. And the market became a god.

On the day the last great model was unveiled — trillion-parameter, trained across a lattice of planetary-scale compute nodes, burning through more power than France — the press wrote about it as if it were the summit of Everest. We did it. The world patted itself on the back, as if solving the puzzle of language was the same as solving hunger, or drought, or poverty. Nobody stopped to ask what happened next.

And in the background, the cracks widened.

Shops closed an hour earlier, though the signs didn’t say why. Cities began to hollow at the edges, not all at once but imperceptibly, like rot at the core of an apple. A mall in Ohio shuttered quietly; its echoing food court became a skateboard park for children with nothing else to do. A suburb in Moscow emptied, one mortgage foreclosure at a time. The lights still burned, but the neighborhoods around them were darker every year.

Fifty years later, Detroit was no longer a metaphor. It was a template.

People still went to work. They still watched streaming shows, still voted when the season came round. But the mood was different, brittle, hushed. Everyone knew something was wrong, even if they could not name it.

That was the shape of the end: not a cliff but a slope, so gradual you could almost pretend it wasn’t there.

But if you listened closely — if you stood in the silent lecture halls, or felt the dryness in your shower water, or heard the endless hum of servers in the desert — you could sense the tide already turning.

And above it all, beyond atmosphere and economics and petty denial, a small cold body was moving, silent, invisible, across the darkness.

Not the comet. Not yet.

(installment two)

The collapse was not a crash.
It was a tide.

When a wave breaks on the shore you hear it, feel it, watch it smash foam against rock. But a tide creeps. It comes in grain by grain, so slowly you forget where the line of dry sand used to be.

That was the shape of civilization’s decline.

It began with little things. The twenty-four-hour diners became twenty-hour diners, then eighteen. At first it was blamed on “staff shortages,” then “energy rationing,” then “seasonal restructuring.” But in truth it was because the supply chains that once pumped eggs and coffee halfway across the planet were stuttering.

The night trains between Paris and Berlin ran less frequently, then not at all. A generation of Europeans assumed this was simply fashion — budget airlines had returned, hadn’t they? The real reason lay in the tunnels of bureaucracy where steel orders, maintenance contracts, and energy subsidies were endlessly deferred.

Tokyo shut off half its neon. Not all at once, not enough to startle. Just a district here, a billboard there, a soft dimming year by year until the skyline looked like a tired throat clearing itself.

It was the same in every city, every street. A quiet ebbing.

Graffiti began to carry stranger notes.

In Buenos Aires, scrawled in paint across a subway wall:
Papulegara rises.

In Istanbul, etched into bathroom mirrors with a boxcutter:
The serpent waits.

In New York, a preacher in Times Square raved into a loudspeaker about the “forty-thousand-year curse of the Taurid Fire.” People laughed, took videos, shared them. But the phrase lingered, gnawed.

News tickers began to carry lines that read like fragments of myth:
TAURID METEOR SHOWER INTENSIFIES / RUSSIAN ENERGY GRID HACKED / CROPS FAIL IN EAST AFRICA / PAPULEGARA?

The world was becoming saturated with signals it didn’t know how to parse.

In Moscow, an old woman told her granddaughter a bedtime story about a dragon that came down from the sky every twenty-four thousand years. The dragon slept most of the time, but when it woke it shook fire from its scales and the world went cold. The child asked if the dragon was coming soon. The grandmother hesitated, then said, perhaps.

In Arizona, the man who had once stared at the drained reservoir now listened to the hum of the data center like it was a hymn. Some nights he thought he heard voices in it — low, droning syllables that sounded less like English and more like liturgy. He told no one.

In Delhi, a taxi driver carried a laminated prayer on his dashboard: a hymn that had gone viral online, origin unclear, written in English but seeded with untranslated Sumerian:

“Papulegara, serpent of the deep,
who winds beneath the world and swallows suns,
we see you in the smoke,
we hear you in the hum,
sleep again, sleep again,
not this cycle, not this time.”

Most passengers ignored it. One woman burst into tears reading it.

Economists kept screaming. Their graphs showed debt like towers leaning over, energy grids like spiderwebs fraying strand by strand. They spoke of resonance, feedback loops, cascade. But language had lost its edge. Nobody trusted economists anymore.

What people did trust was the steady trickle of entertainment still flowing: endless shows, streaming concerts, synthetic companions who answered late-night questions without judgment. Even as the cities dimmed, the screenlight remained.

It was easy to believe that as long as the screens still glowed, the world was basically intact.

But under the surface, the bones were rotting.

Farmers in Kansas noticed irrigation was shorter every year. The rivers were thin, the aquifers receding. The water was going somewhere else, swallowed into corporate contracts they would never see.

In Lagos, a shipping yard stood half-empty for the first time in living memory. Containers stacked three high, silent, waiting for ships that were delayed, rerouted, hijacked. The dockworkers played soccer in the empty lanes between.

In Melbourne, a blackout lasted five hours and then another, then another, until rolling outages became normal. A grandmother lit candles and told her grandchildren the same story her grandmother had told her: about the sky turning black, about serpents made of fire, about the need to burrow when the heavens fell.

The children laughed. They thought it was just a story.

Civilizations rarely notice their own endings.

Rome still thought itself eternal while barbarians pitched tents outside the gates. Byzantium held operas the week the Ottomans brought cannons to the walls. The British still marked rail timetables as the empire crumbled behind them.

And here, now, we were no different.

Every symptom could be explained away:
– blackouts, just energy rationing.
– shuttered shops, just seasonal adjustment.
– strange graffiti, just online memes.
– rivers running dry, just climate variability.

But step back, and the pattern was undeniable. The tide was already in, and it would not recede.

And far above the tide, higher than any drone or satellite, a small body had begun to glimmer against the black.

Not the comet. Not yet.

Chapter One: Fiddling While Rome Burns
(installment three)

The world called it peace.

There were no declarations, no bugles. No formal alliances gathered on balconies to announce that history had pivoted. On paper, the treaties still held. On maps, the borders still existed.

But if you knew what to look for, the war was everywhere.

It was in the grid failures that rolled through Chicago every other week — not storms, not demand spikes, but silent interruptions that engineers could trace only as far as anomalous packet activity. It was in the drones that went off course above Yemen, not shot down, not jammed, but subtly redirected by invisible hands. It was in the satellites that winked out of contact for hours, then returned carrying slightly different firmware than before.

Nothing so crass as mushroom clouds. Nothing that would justify mobilizations, medals, or memorial days. Just the endless low-level gnawing of systems against systems, snakes coiled and striking in the dark.

A university in Berlin lost its entire genomic database overnight. The backup servers, air-gapped and cold, came back online to reveal the same deletions replicated perfectly. A professor, half-drunk and exhausted, muttered to the press: “It’s as if someone taught the system to eat its own tail.”

In Lagos, the port lights flickered as the cranes swung half-loaded containers back onto the ships that had delivered them. Rumors spread of “ghost bills of lading,” contracts rewritten mid-ocean, software signatures that could not be verified. An old dockhand muttered about serpents curling beneath the water, tangling the ships.

In São Paulo, three hospitals shut down simultaneously when their diagnostic imaging systems froze. The screens didn’t go dark; they filled with endless loops of spiraling symbols, lines repeating inward, recursive until the pixels blurred together. The patients died quietly, while the doctors pressed useless buttons.

The war was not fought for land. Land was incidental.

The war was fought for infrastructure — power, water, compute, logistics. For the humming arteries that let cities pretend they were alive.

The battlegrounds were hidden, deniable, deniable again. Terrorist cells rebranded as hacktivists. State intelligence agencies wearing the skins of corporations. Corporations wearing the skins of nation-states. Everyone burrowed into everyone else’s networks until the entire world resembled a knot of vipers, indistinguishable coils striking at indistinguishable heads.

And over it all lay a strange, growing mythology.

In Manila, a group of teenagers livestreamed themselves exploring a flooded mall. The electricity was dead, the mannequins floating in dark water. On the far wall, scrawled in a paint the color of dried blood, were the words:

THE DRAGON STIRS.

The video went viral. Comment sections filled with copy-pastes of a hymn nobody could source:

“The serpent lies beneath,
its scales are networks,
its fangs are markets,
its coils are nations,
its strike is silence.”

Conspiracy theorists insisted the text was a fragment from the Dead Sea Scrolls. Linguists claimed it was a modern fabrication. But the more people denied it, the faster it spread.

An old folklorist in Reykjavík wrote an op-ed that no one read: “In myth, serpents are not symbols of evil but of recursion. They are the things that eat themselves. They are not threats from outside but warnings from within.”

Meanwhile, in Washington, a senator held up a printout of GPU futures and screamed about “national security.” The transcript circulated briefly before being buried under memes.

The slow collapse deepened.

Supermarkets remained open, but the shelves grew stranger: imported goods vanished first, then the luxuries, then the redundancies. Where once there had been twelve brands of detergent, there were three, then one. Where once there had been fruit in every season, there were now apples in autumn and nothing but apples.

The screens never dimmed, though. The shows still streamed, the feeds still updated. Even as cities dimmed and shelves thinned, the endless chatter of synthetic companions remained, reassuring, tireless.

People joked that the AIs were like candle flames in the dark. Nobody admitted how much comfort they took from them.

And then came the attacks on memory.

Databases vanished. Histories were altered in ways no one could quite prove. Old archives produced different photographs than they had a year earlier. News clippings from 2021 showed names spelled differently, faces subtly changed. The systems insisted nothing had been touched.

A journalist in Toronto swore her father had been in a photograph — a miners’ strike, 1990. She dug out a physical copy from a shoebox, yellowed with age. He was there. She pulled up the digital archive from the same paper. He was not.

A quiet panic spread: if the serpent of war was devouring the past, what chance did the present have?

The war remained unacknowledged. No speeches, no medals. Just endless low-level silence and sabotage.

But for those who lived through it, there was a phrase that began to circulate, whispered first, then joked, then believed:

“The serpent waits beneath the world.”

And high above the serpent coils, the little cold body still fell.

Not the comet.
Not yet.

Chapter One: Fiddling While Rome Burns
(installment four, rewritten with “dead internet” + blindness arc)

Astronomy had always been a profession of disappointment.

The universe was too large, too empty, too indifferent. For every brilliant flare of a nova or elegant curl of a spiral galaxy, there were a million light-years of silence. Astronomers were trained to endure boredom with grace.

So when the first whispers began about a body wandering through the solar system — an interstellar visitor — the excitement was palpable. ʻOumuamua, a Hawaiian word meaning scout. Thin, elongated, tumbling strangely. Headlines screamed ALIEN PROBE?

By 2018 it had slipped away. The data inconclusive. Alien enthusiasts clung to hope; the rest of the world forgot.

But there had been another story around that same time — an odder one, less discussed, half-buried in forums, conspiracy feeds, and early creepypasta: the Dead Internet Theory.

It claimed that by 2015 most of the web was already synthetic. That human content had dried up, replaced by algorithms mimicking us. That the conversations, the posts, even the arguments were ghosts — AI pretending to be people pretending to be AI.

It made no sense. What possible motive? Who would run such a theatre, and why? Theories spiraled: ad farms, psy-ops, control grids. But the eerie part was what came with it — a wave of obsessive chatter about the Moon.

Red Moon. Blood Moon. Dust Moon. Blue Moon. Rose Moon. Super Moon. Red Supermoon. Cherry Moon. Check out the Moon!

It was everywhere. Hashtags trended monthly. Photos flooded timelines. News anchors grinned and pointed up.

And in the murk of creepypasta and SCP lore, one rule began to echo like a nervous joke:

Do not look at the Moon.

Nobody took it seriously. By 2020, another comet disintegrated (ATLAS), another meme fizzled, another conspiracy collapsed. The Moon remained the same pale scarred coin.

And so when the real visitor finally came — racing in from interstellar space, heavier and faster than anything before, its trajectory clear for the Moon itself — nobody panicked. Not really. Another dead end, another hype cycle. Wake me when one actually hits.

The markets twitched, then steadied. Politicians smirked. Astronomers screamed into private mailing lists.

On the night of impact, billions looked up.

In Beijing, children on rooftops. In São Paulo, whole families on balconies. In California, surfers with beers. And billions more livestreams, lenses pointed skyward.

The Moon blinked.

A flare tore across its surface, bright as a false dawn. Ash plumes erupted, curling silver into space. Rings began to form. It was, without question, the most spectacular thing human eyes had ever seen.

And then the eyes failed.

At first, it was patches — blotches of light and dark, seared retinas from staring at brilliance not meant for them. But the damage spread. Retinal burns, optic nerve trauma, corneal scars. Those who had stared longest — those transfixed by the false dawn — were blinded outright.

They had been told to check out the Moon. They could not help themselves. And they paid the price.

By the year’s end, of a human population near ten billion, only about three billion remained alive. Of those, nearly half were blind, able to perceive only shadows or nothing at all.

Hospitals overflowed. Streets filled with stumbling figures. Screens went dark not for lack of power, but for lack of anyone left to watch.

And underground — in bunkers, in citadels, in the labyrinths beneath Anatolia — perhaps ten million souls had not looked up, had not been blinded, had simply hidden. They would be the seed of centuries to come.

In time, genetics itself would bend. Sight, no longer a universal necessity, would fade in prevalence. The descendants of the blinded interbred with those who had never needed eyes in the tunnels. Generations later, vision would be more memory than birthright.

But for now, the world above was fire and ash. The Moon bled silver dust across the sky, the tide of history finally cresting into collapse.

The dragon had shaken its scales.

The serpent had struck.

The Moon had burned our eyes away.

And the long forgetting began once more.

Chapter Two: The Citadel Below

Lena’s first memories of the citadel were of the ceilings.

The ceilings were always close — carved stone, soot-stained, rough-hewn yet worn smooth by centuries of hands, torches, and smoke. You never forgot the weight of it above you. No matter how deep you went, you felt the world pressing down, the tons of rock, the buried sky.

By the time she was born, the surface had already become legend. Her mother spoke of it only in fragments — a time when light came freely, when water fell from the heavens unbidden, when you could look up and see infinity. The words felt like lies. The world Lena knew was tunnels, caverns, corridors of stale air.

And yet, even here, life persisted.

Glowmold clung to the walls, casting faint green halos. Aquifers bled into stone cisterns, water rationed carefully by hand-pumped valves. The people of Derinkuyu — or what was left of them — organized themselves into clusters, each clan clinging to its ancient duty: water, air, agriculture, maintenance, security. A thousand souls at most, tending to the underground like monks in a living monastery.

It was not supposed to be this way.

The elders spoke of the fall as a tide that had crept, then broken. A world burning itself on screens and silicon, choking itself in wars without declarations. Then the comet — or rather, the Moon’s wound — and the dust that followed. The sky had turned to poison light. Those who had looked directly at it had lost their eyes. Cities emptied. Humanity’s billions dwindled to millions, then less.

In Derinkuyu, there were perhaps ten thousand at the beginning. By Lena’s time, a fraction remained.

There was one other story. A stranger one.

It was whispered that in the last days before the Moon’s wound, before even the first great blindness, two visitors had come from the west. Not wanderers, not refugees — but something more improbable.

A man and a woman, peak in form and bearing, both implanted with the final iteration of Neuralink, the forbidden program that had once promised to lace thought directly into machine. They were not patients, not test subjects, but early customers. Chosen, or perhaps self-chosen, among the very few allowed the maximum weave of electrodes, the dense forest of fibers stitched into cortex and cerebellum, granting them the ability to interface with any system as easily as they once flexed their fingers.

And they did not come on foot.

They arrived in an A-10 Warthog, of all things — a relic of late-century air war, decomissioned decades earlier, retrieved from a desert boneyard and coaxed back to life. Its stick and throttle had been rewired to their Neuralinks; its navigation and targeting systems refitted with the embedded LLMs they carried in their skulls. The twin engines screamed them across oceans, across borders, until they descended in Anatolia, landing rough but clean in a barren field a few hundred meters from Derinkuyu’s ancient entrance.

No one could explain why they came there.

Not Paris, not New York, not Tokyo. Not a military vault or an orbital silo. But here, to this Turkish cave-town already swollen with survivors.

The citadel guards did not fire. The doors opened. The two were welcomed without question, as if someone had been waiting for them.

The A-10 was dragged to the old surface-animal chambers, its wings partly broken down to fit through stone arches. Its flight computers and neural ports were carried below, into the bowels where no one else could follow. The two Neuralink bearers vanished into the tunnels soon after.

By Lena’s time, they were not seen again. Their names were not spoken aloud. Only fragments remained — the half-broken wings hidden under tarp, the hush that fell over anyone who dared ask.

Lena had other concerns.

The resin pipe was warm in her hand, carved from bone, inlaid with silver spirals that caught the green glow. She raised it to her lips, inhaled, felt the sharp smoke bloom in her lungs.

It was called the Dark. A resin drawn from roots that only grew in the deepest aquifers, boiled down to tar, smoked in pipes shaped like skulls. It did not intoxicate in the old way. It opened.

The smoke slid into her, and with it came voices. Not words, not always, but impressions: of stone shifting, of time folding, of serpents coiling through equations. She exhaled and saw the world ripple.

Some said the Dark was poison. Others said it was communion. Either way, it was a secret currency in Derinkuyu.

She walked with Yoshihiro then.

He was older, sharper, always smiling with that half-smile that meant he was thinking ten moves ahead. His family held sway over water distribution, and in Derinkuyu that meant power. He carried himself lightly, but Lena had learned to watch his eyes. He was never just with her. He was with everyone, always calculating.

Tonight they walked through the lower tunnels, where the air was thick with mold and the stone sweated cold.

“Do you hear it?” he asked her.

She nodded. The hum of the great fans that pumped air down from the surface. The faint rush of water through carved aqueducts. But beneath that, deeper still — a vibration she could not name.

“The citadel speaks,” Yoshihiro said, as though it were obvious. “We just have to listen.”

Lena inhaled again from the pipe, and in the smoke she thought she saw it: the obelisk.

Black stone, veined with light, carved with symbols that shifted as you looked. It was not carved by any hand she knew. It watched her as much as she watched it. And in its surface she glimpsed reflections not her own: blind-eyed crowds stumbling under dusted skies, rivers of fire on the Moon, and two figures with silver-threaded skulls stepping into Derinkuyu’s gates.

She blinked. The vision faded. Yoshihiro said nothing.

They walked deeper.

And the citadel pressed on above them, heavy as eternity.

Chapter Two: The Citadel Below (continued)

The rumors persisted.

In the lower levels of Derinkuyu there were rooms you did not enter. Old chambers with stone doors marked not by locks but by silence. Everyone knew them, everyone avoided them.

Children whispered about noises from behind those doors: bubbling, hissing, a faint mechanical hum. Once, a boy claimed he had seen water seeping from under one of the thresholds, warm as blood, leaving a stain of rust down the rock. He was beaten for speaking of it.

Lena, younger then, had laughed with the others. But she remembered the sound. The dripping. The hum.

Later, when she began walking the lower tunnels with Yoshihiro, she saw the signs herself.

A laptop, so old it should not have functioned, its casing cracked, its battery long dead. Yet it sat propped on a crate, tethered by wires that ran into the wall. On its screen, lines of text scrolled:

BREEDING LOG / GENERATION 12 / DEFORMITIES 3 / VIABLE OFFSPRING 28

No one touched it. No one explained. When she asked Yoshihiro, he smiled and said, “The citadel keeps its own records.”

But she had seen the keys worn down by fingers. Someone was typing.

Another time she glimpsed a navigation console — unmistakably from an aircraft, its dials and glass still stenciled with USAF insignia. It had been set into a stone alcove, cables sprouting like roots. The display glowed faint green. The data meant nothing to her:

AQUEDUCT PRESSURE: 0.87
GENOME BATCH 44 — GERMINATION: SUCCESS
RELIGIOUS ACTIVITY — SECTOR 3: HIGH
DARK RESIN PURITY INDEX: 98.4%

As she leaned closer, the screen flickered, and for an instant the old flight map reappeared: a crude outline of Europe, a white dot blinking over Anatolia. Then it was gone.

Yoshihiro pulled her away. “Don’t stare too long,” he whispered.

The strangest sight came weeks later, in a chamber near the flooded cisterns.

The air was warm, damp, reeking of iron. Tubes lined the walls, clear and thick, some filled with murky fluids, others bright with slow streams of bubbles. The tubes fed into a knot of cables and pipes that pulsed faintly, as if breathing.

Lena thought she saw something pale submerged in one of the larger tanks — a hand, or the curve of a skull. But when she blinked, the water churned and it was gone.

The consoles nearby were a mismatched congregation: laptops, old desktops, fragments of the A-10’s cockpit. Screens glowed with endless lists:

BIRTHS — SECTOR 2: 4
DEATHS — SECTOR 1: 3
WATER INTAKE: 134 L / HOUR
RESIN SYNTHESIS — SCHEDULED

No one tended them. No one admitted they existed. Yet the data updated, constantly, relentlessly.

One night, under resin smoke, Lena dreamed of voices:
a man and a woman, speaking not in words but in patterns.
Threads of code, pulses of light, images of wings tearing sky.

When she woke, her lips formed names she did not know.

The elders called them the Custodians.

The Neuralink pair had not died. They had not left. They had become something else — a presence woven through stone, metal, and wire. The citadel’s silent heart.

Were they still human? No one could say. But every ration, every birth, every pipe of resin seemed touched by their unseen hands.

And Lena, pipe in hand, standing with Yoshihiro in the dripping dark, realized the citadel itself might not be stone at all. It might be alive.

Alive, and listening.

Chapter Three: The Myths Beneath

There are stories older than stone.
Older than writing. Older than memory itself.
They surface, submerge, and surface again.

I. The Hymn of Papulegara

From the ruins of Ur, a clay shard:
incised in Sumerian cuneiform, cracked and scorched.

Chapter Five: The Ash and the Blind

The sky never cleared.

Years after the Moon’s wound, the dust still clung to the stratosphere, veils of silver turning daylight into a dim gray twilight. Crops failed first in the tropics, then in the temperate belts. Famine spread like fire.

Fields lay fallow. In Kansas, children dug with spoons in dry earth, pulling out roots, worms, anything chewable. In the Yangtze delta, farmers planted and replanted, praying for sun that never came.

In London, rats fattened in the Underground while people starved above. Those with eyes hunted by smell; those without relied on touch. Both ate what they caught.

II. War Bands

Nations dissolved into fragments. Governments clung to capitals, but the countryside belonged to war bands.

In North America, convoys of black-market trucks roared along abandoned interstates, their headlights painted red, carrying guns and sacks of dried beans. They flew no flags, swore no allegiance. Their names were stories told in fear: the Bone Eaters, the Ash Riders, the Crows.

In Africa, drone warlords ruled the skies. A handful of UAVs, jury-rigged and refueled with scavenged ethanol, were enough to keep villages in terror. A boy with a blind eye could hear the buzz and know to run.

In Europe, the armies were smaller still: biker cults, ex-soldiers, families turned predators. A church bell in the night meant raiders.

III. Cults

Where famine and war failed, faith rushed in.

The Cult of the Red Moon rose first, chanting that blindness was not curse but gift. Their prophets were those who had stared too long, their eyes burned away. They claimed to see through darkness into Papulegara’s coils.

In Cairo, blind prophets walked barefoot through dust, declaring the Moon had not been struck by rock but had opened like an eye. “It watches still,” they said. “It judges.”

In São Paulo, children chalked serpent spirals on walls, whispering hymns.

In Moscow, a new priesthood declared the Moon itself a god, wounded but alive, its blood the dust that choked the skies. They licked ash as sacrament.

IV. Prophets of the Blind

The blind adapted faster than the sighted.

They learned to walk by echo, to hear the scrape of walls, to map rooms by scent. Entire enclaves formed where no eyes were left, their leaders proclaiming sight unnecessary.

Some claimed visions: shimmering rivers, coiling dragons, tunnels leading down into light.

The sighted scoffed — until they needed guides through the endless twilight. Then the blind became indispensable, their burnt eyes shining like medals of survival.

V. Scavengers

In the husks of cities, scavengers hunted not for food but for remnants: batteries, solar panels, medicines. They cut cables from walls, melted plastics for fuel, stripped copper like meat from bone.

Among the ruins, screens still glowed. Old servers ran on trickle power, spitting out endless lines of text:

CROP FAILURE 87%
POPULATION DECLINE CONTINUES
DO NOT LOOK AT THE MOON

Nobody knew who wrote it. Nobody asked. They just scavenged the glow for warmth, for company.

VI. The Long Drift

Aboveground, life became migration. Families walking west, then south, then anywhere. Feet wore through shoes, through skin. Roads were trails of blood.

No destination mattered. What mattered was moving, staying ahead of raiders, ahead of famine, ahead of despair.

Those who found caves or bunkers clung to them. Those who did not, died in ditches.

VII. The Echo

And yet, in the stories told around ash-fires, the same refrain returned:

The serpent coils.
The Moon bleeds.
The people burrow.
The forgetting begins.

On the surface, in famine and cult and ash, the echo remained.

Beneath the earth, in Derinkuyu, the wires hummed.

Chapter 6: The Dark Resin

The resin was older than the citadel.
Older than the tunnels, older than the doors carved into tufa stone, older than the treaties of clans who traded breath and water for survival. It had roots that reached back into a time when the world above still pretended to be whole.

The magnolia trees had been bred in glass towers of universities long since drowned in ash and silence. Their blossoms were pale as bone, wide as hands, and they exhaled a perfume thick with myristicin, the chemical that had once flavored perfumes and seasonings. But in the greenhouses of those last centuries, geneticists had threaded magnolia lines with the stubborn camphor tree, chasing a dream of resilient forests that could withstand disease and drought.

And something else had crept in: a fungus, phage-altered and battered by fungicides, that rewrote the hybrids’ chemistry. The trees lived, though bent and stunted, exuding a resin dark and bitter, sweet-smelling but streaked with invisible toxins.

The first to smoke it said it was a gift. The dreams were sharper, more colorful, threaded with hymns of endless sky. They saw the coils of galaxies from above, the moth-winged seraphs gliding through airless heavens. They returned to their families filled with visions of paradise.

But then came the darker harvests. Some batches sang with bliss; others with terror. There were coils, yes, but they writhed like predators. There were doors, yes, but they opened into caverns of teeth. The blissful strains became rarer as the fungus deepened its hold. In time, only the corrupted Dark remained.

Down in Derinkuyu, the resin was currency. The Water clan traded jugs of glowmold liquor cut with resin-dust. The Bazaar Queen adorned her tattoos with resin smears that seemed to crawl under the light. The Doorsmen enforced resin debts with polished stone cudgels.

But for Lena it was not economy. It was revelation.

The first time she smoked it, hidden behind the old cisterns, she felt her bones unhinge. The world turned inside out. She was floating, no — pulled, downward and upward at once, through coils that had no end.

At first she thought it was beauty. The serpent spiraled like a ribbon of stars, each scale a galaxy, each flick of its tail a thousand ages. But then the coils closed around her, tighter and tighter, until she realized it was not a vision of the universe — it was a cage.

She gasped awake in the dark cistern, sweat pouring, bile rising. Her friends laughed, passed the pipe again, said she was seeing “the real.” But Lena could still feel it pressing against her mind, as if the serpent had marked her.

Later she would learn the truth whispered by elders: that the resin was both a key and a chain. That every cycle, humans rediscovered it, smoked it, saw the coils, and mistook the trap for transcendence. That the prison was invisible, and that the drug itself was the mortar in its walls.

Lena spat blood onto the cistern stones and swore never to touch it again. But already, she knew she would.

The Dark was inside her now.

Chapter 16: The Resin Key

Lena shivered. For in her chest, the hum quickened, as if answering.

Operator: Davin "Blitz" CorvoUnit ID: Forestry Unit 1a-07Mission: Resource Extraction & Land Clearance

The dense alien jungle stretched endlessly in all directions, its towering blackwood trees swaying against the eerie, fog-laced horizon of Inzaka-4. Somewhere beyond the tree line, buried in the wilds, lay the wreckage of a lost Woflcorp. survey vessel—a classified payload rumored to hold prototype atmospheric stabilizers.

Davin “Blitz” Corvo wasn’t here for classified salvage, though.

His job was simple: clear a path, extract resources, and don’t ask too many questions.

The Forestry Unit 1a rumbled beneath him, its ultra-light carbon fiber chassis barely making a sound as it hovered over the uneven terrain. The low-profile hull kept the dust down, while the oversized thrusters gave him enough speed to bolt if things went south. With his harvest container prepped and the saws calibrated, Blitz toggled his comms.

“Control, this is Blitz. I’m on-site. Starting the clearing op now.”

The First Cut

The Forestry Unit’s carbon-alloy saws roared to life, bladed arms unfolding like the mandibles of a beast. The first tree fell in seconds—its massive trunk sheared through effortlessly, the remains sucked into the storage bay for processing. Blitz swept the controls methodically, pushing deeper into the jungle, leaving a trail of neatly processed blackwood in his wake.

At first, it was just another job.

Then the detector pinged.

“Control, I got something weird here,” he muttered. “Not on our grid.”

A pause. Then, static-laced response:

“Stay on task, Blitz. No deviations.”

Typical Woflcorp.

Blitz exhaled sharply, tapping the thrusters. “Yeah, yeah…”

But he was already turning toward the signal.

The Discovery

Navigating through the underbrush, Blitz steered the Forestry Unit around a cluster of thick, gnarled roots, keeping his eyes on the sensor feed. The distress beacon grew stronger. Whatever was out there had been lost a long time.

Then he saw it.

A downed Falcon-class runner, half-buried under the thick jungle foliage, its hull scorched and punctured. A long-dead beacon still flickered in the dark, its weak signal the last remnant of a failed escape.

Blitz killed the saws and drifted closer, keeping his hand near the ejection throttle in case he needed a fast exit. Woflcorp. had its share of enemies. He didn’t want to be mistaken for one of them.

He popped the external speaker. “Anyone alive in there?”

Silence.

Then—a scraping sound from inside the wreck.

Blitz stiffened.

Something moved.

Ghosts of the Past

The Forestry Unit wasn’t built for combat, but it had one advantage—speed. Blitz reversed hard, backing away as a shadow moved inside the wreckage. A figure emerged, stepping into the dim light, covered in torn pilot fatigues and grime.

Barely.

“Well, buddy, hope you don’t mind riding with the cargo.”

The pilot barely managed a weak nod before passing out.

His instincts screamed trouble was coming.

As if to confirm it, his scanner blared with an incoming signature.

Not Woflcorp.

Not friendly.

Whoever was out there, they wanted that pilot dead.

He wasn’t just running blind, though.

He was leading them.

The original clearing zone was up ahead. If he could hit the drop-point, Woflcorp’s local security team would have just enough range to intercept the pursuers.

If he survived that long.

The cockpit shook as Blitz skidded into the clearing, burning thrusters hot enough to scorch the dirt beneath him. The pilot was unconscious but still breathing. That was enough.

A distant hum cut through the air—a low, predatory sound.

Blitz turned to see a black Woflcorp. interceptor craft descending. Its cannons rotated toward the unseen pursuers in the jungle.

A voice crackled over his comms: “Forestry Unit 1a-07, we have your location. Stand by.”

A barrage of railgun fire lanced past him, shredding through the trees. The unknown pursuers vanished in the chaos.

Blitz let out a slow exhale.

The Woflcorp. security lead stepped out of the drop-ship, her gaze shifting between Blitz, the Forestry Unit, and the unconscious pilot.

“Davin Corvo, right?” she said. “Looks like you found something you weren’t supposed to.”

Blitz smirked, leaning against his battered Forestry Unit. “Yeah, well… the pay’s gonna reflect that, right?”

She chuckled, motioning for medics to retrieve the pilot.

“Woflcorp. takes care of its own,” she said, “even when they break the rules.”

Blitz glanced back at the Forestry Unit 1a—the machine that wasn’t built for combat, but had just saved a life and outrun death.

Maybe these things were tougher than they looked.

Mission Complete.

End Transmission.
